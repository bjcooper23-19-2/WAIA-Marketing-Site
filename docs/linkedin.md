# WAIA LinkedIn Guidance

This document defines how WAIA's wider doctrine should be expressed through LinkedIn content, comments and engagement.

It is subordinate to [`docs/waia-beliefs.md`](./waia-beliefs.md). If a LinkedIn tactic conflicts with the belief system, the belief system wins.

## Role Of LinkedIn

LinkedIn should help WAIA build a recognisable point of view on the transformation of work.

The objective is not to become another account that comments on every AI announcement. The stronger position is to help people make sense of what changing technology means for real work, managers, teams, workflows and business decisions.

WAIA should sound like an experienced operator looking at change through **People. Process. Data.**

## Core LinkedIn Themes

Content and comments should usually connect to one or more of these themes:

### People

- changing roles and expectations
- judgement and accountability
- confidence and capability
- management behaviour
- trust and autonomy
- culture and psychological safety around experimentation
- learning, deskilling and dependency
- how change feels at different levels of the organisation

### Process

- workflow redesign
- checking and rework
- hand-offs and bottlenecks
- experimentation becoming repeatable practice
- automation after process clarity
- standardisation without unnecessary bureaucracy
- capacity release and redeployment
- where human review belongs

### Data

- evidence of better work
- activity versus effectiveness
- credible productivity measurement
- capacity signals
- organisational learning
- what should be standardised, stopped or scaled
- management decisions supported by evidence

## Comment Target Selection

Prioritise posts where WAIA can add a useful operating perspective rather than simply agree with the author.

Strong targets include discussions about:

- productivity and efficiency
- workplace transformation
- management and leadership
- changing job roles
- culture and trust
- skills and learning
- automation and agents
- governance and controls
- workflow redesign
- decision quality
- capacity and resource allocation
- adoption claims and usage data
- ROI or time-saved claims
- human review and accountability
- operating models

Do not restrict target selection to posts that repeatedly use the word **AI**. Relevant conversations about work, management, process, productivity and culture may be better opportunities to express the doctrine.

Avoid over-indexing on:

- generic launch announcements
- prompt-tip content
- tool directories
- novelty demonstrations with no operating implication
- engagement bait
- posts where the only useful response would be praise or repetition

## Comment Lens

Before commenting, ask:

- What changed in the work?
- What changed for the person doing it?
- What happened elsewhere in the workflow?
- Was capacity genuinely released or was effort moved?
- Where does judgement sit?
- What does this ask of managers?
- What evidence would make the claim more credible?
- Is this an experiment, or a repeatable way of working?
- Does this strengthen or weaken trust and culture?

A comment does not need to answer all of these. One useful angle is usually stronger than trying to force the whole doctrine into a reply.

## Tone

Comments should feel natural, experienced and specific.

Prefer:

- calm disagreement where useful
- practical observations
- one clear idea
- language that sounds like a real operator
- short paragraphs
- direct references to the work or workflow

Avoid:

- formulaic thought-leadership phrasing
- forced questions designed only to drive engagement
- repeating the author's post back to them
- excessive jargon
- lecturing
- turning every comment into a WAIA pitch
- using the same signature phrase so often that it becomes mechanical

The doctrine should be visible in the thinking, not pasted into every comment.

## Content Strategy

WAIA and personal-account content can explore the same worldview from different angles.

A healthy content mix should rotate across:

- a clear point of view
- practical operating advice
- evidence and research
- challenge to a common assumption
- observations about management and culture
- workflow and process examples
- reflections on capacity and business value
- lessons from how organisations are adapting

Do not publish a sequence where every post is simply another version of "AI adoption is not value". The doctrine is broader than that.

## Language Principle

Use **AI** when it is genuinely the subject or when market/search language requires it.

When the subject is work, use the language of work.

Prefer natural references to:

- transformation of work
- workflows
- new ways of working
- management
- judgement
- capacity
- evidence
- operating practices
- automation
- culture
- organisational learning

Do not use vague substitutes simply to avoid the term AI. Clarity still matters.

## Commercial Boundary

LinkedIn should build recognition and credibility before it tries to sell.

When WAIA is mentioned, the connection should be natural and relevant to the point being made.

Avoid turning every discussion into:

- a demo request
- a product pitch
- a feature explanation
- a claim that WAIA solves the entire problem

The stronger long-term signal is that WAIA consistently helps people think more clearly about how work is changing.

## Decision Test

Before posting or commenting, check:

1. Does this add a useful perspective rather than simply add noise?
2. Does it connect to People, Process or Data?
3. Is the point about real work rather than technology activity for its own sake?
4. Is the language natural rather than doctrinal or over-produced?
5. Is any material claim credible and proportionate?
6. Would the comment or post still be worthwhile if WAIA were not mentioned?

If the answer is mostly no, it is probably not worth publishing.
